# State of the Art: Amazon Bioeconomy

*Evidence-Based Curatorial Synthesis*

🌐 Languages: [PT](estado-da-arte.md) · **EN** · [ES](estado-da-arte-es.md)

## Synthesis

This synthesis organizes the documentary corpus in the `bioeconomia-amazonica` collection into six thematic axes, covering the 2004–2026 period. The scope encompasses biochar and pyrogenic carbon, Amazonian Dark Earth and tree growth, forest fires and forest integrity in Acre, deforestation and population sustainability, measurement of the Brazilian bioeconomy, sociobioeconomy and social technology, digital platforms for non-timber forest product chains, and integrated production systems in the eastern Amazon. The writing follows the Repository's rule: **do not fabricate data** — every number, author, and year cited comes from a record; curatorial interpretations are identified as such. The collection rigorously distinguishes the nature of its sources: narrative and systematic reviews, mathematical modeling, institutional documentary records, and empirical field data support claims of differing weights, and the text states this hierarchy in each section.

## Executive Summary

- ✓ **Convergent evidence (empirical)** — recurrent fire reduces the tree component and favors bamboo dominance in Acre's open forests: culm density seven to nine times higher in the dissertation by [Silva (2017)](silva-2017-dinamica-incendios-acre.md) and, in the article by [Silva et al. (2017)](silva-et-al-2017-incendios-dominancia-bambu.md), from two to approximately fifteen culms per 25 m², with tree mortality increasing from 8% to 50% and a reduction from about 300 to 100 individuals per 0,5 hectare.
- ✓ **Convergent evidence (independent reviews)** — biochar quality (carbon stability, O/C and H/Corg ratios, porosity) is a function of feedstock and pyrolysis conditions, with convergence between the narrative review by [Matarru and Shin (2025)](ficha-biochar-negative-emissions-review.md) and the technical-scientific chapter by [Maia et al. (2021)](maia-biochar-eucalipto-2021.md).
- ⚠ **Moderate evidence (single field experiment)** — Amazonian Dark Earth used as a bio-inoculant for seedlings increased field growth over 180 days: ~20% greater height in *Schizolobium amazonicum* and ~55% in *Handroanthus avellanedae* ([de Freitas et al. 2026](dark-earths-crescimento-arvores-2026.md)) — single dose, one site, no long-term follow-up.
- ⚠ **Moderate evidence (local scope)** — integrated systems with a native tree component (babassu) improved soil chemical indicators (surface organic matter, pH, lower potential acidity) in Pindaré-Mirim, Maranhão, without universal superiority of any system ([Souza et al. 2024](souza-et-al-2024-solo-sistemas-integrados.md)).
- ⚠ **Moderate evidence (documentary record)** — the Brazilian bioeconomy is measurable at the macro scale: US$ 285,9 billion in biomass-attributable sales in 2016 (13,8% of that year's GDP as an order-of-magnitude comparison) — **attributed sales are not equivalent to GDP, value added, or socioenvironmental benefit** ([Silva, Pereira, and Martins 2018](silva-pereira-martins-2018-bioeconomia-numeros.md)).
- ⚠ **Moderate evidence (theoretical-empirical, single case)** — sociobioeconomy can be analytically operationalized through a social technological system, applied to the rubber chain in Pará ([Rodrigues et al. 2024](sociobioeconomia-tecnologia-social-amazonia-2024.md)).
- ⚠ **Moderate evidence (systematic review)** — digital platforms for non-timber forest products have a proposed four-pillar framework, **not empirically tested** by the authors themselves ([Silva et al. 2025](plataformas-digitais-bioeconomia-amazonica-2025.md)).
- ✗ **No evidence** — effectiveness of specific technologies (bamboo, biochar, agroforestry systems) inferred from aggregate modeling: [Bologna and Aquino (2020)](bologna-aquino-2020-desmatamento-populacao.md) does not demonstrate any technology; the probability below 10% of avoiding collapse is a **model result**, not an empirical forecast.
- ✗ **No evidence** — financial viability of carbon projects in restoration: [Martins (2004)](martins-2004-carbono-matas-ciliares.md) records insufficient revenue under 2004 assumptions (US$ 5 per certificate against an estimated cost of US$ 7,20 per tCO₂ equivalent), and the values are not transferable to current markets or to bamboo.
- ✗ **No evidence** — bamboo abundance as a sustainable surplus for exploitation: post-fire *Guadua* expansion is a sign of disturbance and structural change, not ecological availability ([Silva 2017](silva-2017-dinamica-incendios-acre.md); [Silva et al. 2017](silva-et-al-2017-incendios-dominancia-bambu.md)).

## 2. Biochar and pyrogenic carbon

### 2.1. Thermochemical routes and quality indicators

The [review by Matarru and Shin (2025)](ficha-biochar-negative-emissions-review.md) (*Energies*, vol. 18, no. 18, art. 4898) is a **narrative review** — as the record itself notes, it does not report bibliographic databases, search strategy, period covered, inclusion criteria, or formal quality assessment, and **must not be described as a systematic review**. It maps thermochemical routes: slow pyrolysis favors solid retention; gasification prioritizes gas production; hydrothermal carbonization (HTC) processes wet biomass without intensive pre-drying. Carbon stability, O/C and H/Corg ratios, and porosity are treated as key indicators for sequestration and applications, which include soil conditioning, adsorption, construction materials, energy production, and hydrogen pathways. The review recognizes risks and costs — scale, logistics, feedstock cost, storage, market price, contaminants, and heavy metals — and makes broad adoption conditional on robust standards, interdisciplinary research, and public policies. As the record notes, **negative-emissions potential depends on LCA boundaries, permanence, coproduct use, and the sustainable origin of biomass**, and the review neither demonstrates the performance of a specific kiln nor authorizes carbon-credit claims without a dedicated methodology.

### 2.2. The Brazilian state of the art with eucalyptus

The [chapter by Maia et al. (2021)](maia-biochar-eucalipto-2021.md) (Embrapa Florestas, chap. 13, pp. 589–610) is a **technical-scientific review with research data from Embrapa and partners, without a declared systematic protocol**. It defines biochar as a heterogeneous matrix rich in aromatic carbon, produced through pyrolysis of sustainably sourced biomass for agricultural, livestock, or forestry use (definitions from the European Biochar Foundation, 2013, and the International Biochar Initiative, 2015), distinguishing it from fuel charcoal. It locates the conceptual origin in Amazonian **Dark Earths** — anthropogenic soils with a high fraction of pyrogenic carbon (charcoal fragments, often at depths exceeding 2 m) and fertility far greater than adjacent soils.

Data compiled by the source: eucalyptus lignin content ranges from 20,2% (*E. smithii*) to 31,7% (*E. alba*), averaging 26%; lignin-rich biomass produces higher-quality charcoal and a greater solid yield. Pyrolysis temperature determines the product: *E. dunni* and *E. urophylla* biochars at 550 °C had elemental carbon contents of 78,14% and 81,94% (versus 70,61% and 67,52% at 350 °C), with a decline in oxygen — the higher the temperature, the lower the O/C ratio and the degree of functionalization; less-functionalized biochar is more adsorptive, whereas more-functionalized biochar interacts more with nutrients (CEC).

Trials reported by the source: in common bean (Embrapa Rice and Beans, without mineral nitrogen), any biochar dose reduced soil aluminum content, 8 Mg/ha increased phosphorus, and 32 Mg/ha increased yield and potassium concentrations; in seedlings, treatments with 75% and 100% activated biochar (water vapor >400 °C) produced the best height and root-collar diameter results in eucalyptus, and there was a significant increase in *Tachigali vulgaris*, whereas one study with *E. dunnii* found no differences; in a Paraná plantation (*urograndis*, 3,2 Mg/ha in a Red-Yellow Oxisol, São Jerônimo da Serra-PR), biochar with NPK fertilization increased soil urease enzyme activity after 3 months, reduced soil bulk density, and increased total porosity, but through 18 months biochar application alone **did not influence height/diameter** — it only indicated the potential to reduce the commercial fertilizer dose by 20% — and caused neither escape nor mortality of *Eisenia andrei* earthworms.

The source identifies key gaps: analytical methods quantify pyrogenic carbon without distinguishing it from humified organic matter; an interlaboratory study (COST action TD1107) in which **only pH and carbon showed acceptable performance among 38 attributes** (Bachmann et al., 2016); the absence of Brazilian quality criteria (minimum carbon content, ash, pollutants), reference materials, and regulation; and the need for long-term studies, economic assessment, and life-cycle assessment.

### 2.3. Status of this axis

**Moderate confidence** (convergent reviews on quality indicators); **insufficient evidence for carbon-credit claims or performance in a specific territory**. The records support that biochar quality is a function of feedstock and process, and that biochar has potential in soil conditioning, adsorption, construction materials, and energy — always conditional on LCA boundaries, permanence, biomass origin, and quality standards. The axis **does not support**: long-term field effectiveness of biochar (the reported trials extend to 18 months); compliance with Brazilian criteria (which do not exist in the source); or automatic equivalence between biochar production and accountable negative emissions.

## 3. Amazonian Dark Earth, microbiome, and tree growth

The [article by de Freitas et al. (2026)](dark-earths-crescimento-arvores-2026.md) (*BMC Ecology and Evolution*, 26:15) is a **field experiment** — according to the record, the first field test in forest restoration of a small volume of Amazonian Dark Earth (ADE) as a microbial bio-inoculant. The site was Embrapa's experimental area in Itacoatiara-AM (a 1,2 ha former cassava plot surrounded by native forest; Oxisol; Amw climate, mean annual temperature of 28 °C, annual precipitation of 1.750–2,500 mm). Seedlings were produced in 290 cm³ tubes with 290 g of ADE (treatment) or coconut fiber (~0,72 g/cm³, control), in randomized blocks, with 6 plants per treatment as replicates, 72 plants in total, no fertilizers, establishment in April 2023, and measurements at 180 days.

Results: all plants survived; *Schizolobium amazonicum* grew ~20% more in height (p < 0,05) and had a ~15% larger stem; *Handroanthus avellanedae* grew ~55% more in height and had a ~88% larger stem. ADE was more fertile than the soil in the experimental area (phosphorus 156,7 ± 33,0 vs. 3,8 ± 1,9 mg/dm³; calcium 6,7 ± 1,8 vs. 1,0 ± 0,5 cmolc/dm³; carbon 25,6 ± 5,6 vs. 21,0 ± 4,6 g/kg). The identified mechanism was restructuring of the rhizosphere microbiome: in *S. amazonicum*, ADE restructured the fungal community and suppressed genera such as *Penicillium*, *Myrothecium*, and yeasts; in *H. avellanedae*, it depleted 17 bacterial genera — including potential pathogens (*Pseudoxanthomonas*) and the nitrogen fixer *Rhizobium* — and opportunistic/pathogenic fungi (*Lasiodiplodia*, *Exophiala*, *Diaporthe*), while promoting biocontrol and growth-promoting agents (*Metarhizium*, *Tomentella*, *Curvularia*, *Humicola*). Co-occurrence networks in *H. avellanedae* with ADE showed a strong increase in connectivity (898 edges, mean degree 18,9, nonmodular network).

The study raises, as an observation by the authors, a **negative feedback**: ADE's high nutrient content may reduce the need for microbial functions such as nitrogen fixation — challenging the prevailing view that ADE's effect depends equally on nutrients and microbiota. Limitations declared in the record: short evaluation period (180 days), a single dose and application method, limited replication (6 plants per treatment per block), and management and preservation issues concerning archaeological/anthropogenic sites **not discussed in the article**.

**Cross-axis interface:** the record connects this axis to the structural-bamboo axis and the Repository's biochar line through the concept of pyrogenic carbon — the same anthropogenic soil that inspired the concept of biochar (documented in [Maia et al. 2021](maia-biochar-eucalipto-2021.md)) is demonstrated here as a living microbiological inoculant in species of restoration interest; the connection is thematic and conceptual, with no transfer of results to bamboo as a construction component. **Status of this axis — moderate confidence** (single, replicable field experiment, with open data in SRA and GitHub): supports the potential of ADE as a restoration inoculant and the microbiological mechanism; **does not support** long-term effects, scaling, or site management.

## 4. Fires, deforestation, and forest integrity

### 4.1. Fire dynamics in Acre (INPA dissertation)

The [dissertation by Silva (2017)](silva-2017-dinamica-incendios-acre.md) (INPA, supervised by Philip Martin Fearnside, co-supervised by Paulo Maurício Lima de Alencastro Graça; defended on August 17, 2017) combines remote sensing (Landsat 5, 7, and 8, 1984–2016; fire scars identified with the Burn Scar Index in CLASlite; weights of evidence in Dinamica EGO) and forest inventories in intact areas, once-burned areas, and areas affected by recurrence (the 2005 and 2010 fires). Over the 33-year period, it estimated **5.251 km² of forest affected by fire in Acre** — 3.883 km² affected once, 598 km² twice, and 57 km² three times — with fragmentation and proximity to pasture and roads among the main associated factors.

In the inventories, fire reduced tree density, species richness, and aboveground dry biomass; **bamboo culm density was seven to nine times higher than in intact forest**, with bamboo expansion linearly related to the reduction in tree numbers and associated with damaged or broken trees. The author estimated **committed emissions of 38 Tg of CO₂** from the reduction in forest biomass through 2016. As the record notes, the dissertation **does not reduce bamboo occurrence to fire**: it distinguishes its natural dynamics from the additional effects produced by fires. Limitations: observational comparisons between burned and unburned conditions; a bounded set of areas and plots; estimates conditional on the equations and spatial limits of the study.

### 4.2. Fires and bamboo dominance in eastern Acre (SBSR article)

The [article by Silva et al. (2017)](silva-et-al-2017-incendios-dominancia-bambu.md) (XVIII SBSR, Santos-SP, pp. 5605–5611) is a specific segment of Sonaira Silva's doctoral research: open forest along the AC-90 highway in Rio Branco. Using Landsat TM5 imagery from 2005 and 2010 processed in CLASlite and a 2016 field inventory, it compared three conditions (unburned forest, forest burned in 2005, and forest burned in 2005 and 2010), with three 100 m × 50 m plots per condition and 5 m × 5 m subplots for culm counts.

Results: burned areas showed higher values and a narrower NDVI range, interpreted as a more homogeneous canopy; the mean increased from **two culms per 25 m² in unburned forest to approximately fifteen culms per 25 m² in burned areas**; the number of trees fell from about 300 to 100 per 0,5 hectare, and the mortality rate increased from 8% to 50%. The authors conclude that bamboo occupancy expands after one and two fire occurrences and formulate the **hypothesis** that areas classified as open forest with nondominant bamboo may be transforming into open forest with dominant bamboo — a hypothesis that, as the record notes, **requires confirmation**. Declared limitations: sampling concentrated in eastern Acre, observational comparisons without random assignment, a significance level of p < 0,1 in some comparisons, and differences among Landsat sensors; **higher NDVI does not, by itself, signify ecological recovery** — in the study, it accompanies homogenization and greater bamboo occupancy.

### 4.3. Deforestation and population sustainability (modeling)

The [article by Bologna and Aquino (2020)](bologna-aquino-2020-desmatamento-populacao.md) (*Scientific Reports*, vol. 10, art. 7631) is **mathematical modeling and simulation**, not an empirical survey: generalized logistic equations for population and forest resources, a dichotomous stochastic process for technological evolution (with energy consumption as a proxy for technological level), numerical integration using Euler's method, and trajectory simulations up to the "point of no return." Under the adopted scenarios and assumptions, the probability of avoiding catastrophic collapse remains **below 10% even in the most optimistic estimate**, with mean times to the point of no return on the order of a few decades in trajectories that do not avoid collapse. The source itself acknowledges that the model does not specify the concrete technological mechanism that would replace forest consumption. As the record notes, the figure must be presented as a **model result, not an observed probability for humanity's future**, and the article does not demonstrate the effectiveness of bamboo, biochar, agroforestry systems, or any specific technology.

### 4.4. Status of this axis

**Moderate to high confidence** for the fire → *Guadua* expansion phenomenon in Acre (two independent records — dissertation and article — with complementary methods and convergent results); **low** for global projections (Bologna and Aquino 2020 modeling); **insufficient evidence** to claim that post-fire bamboo abundance constitutes an exploitable resource. The records support that recurrent fire degrades the forest (loss of biomass, mortality, canopy homogenization) and that abundant bamboo may be a **sign of disturbance and structural change**, not evidence of sustainable surplus — a direct warning for territorial assessments proposing bamboo production chains in the Amazon.

**Cross-axis interface:** the two fire records connect this axis to the structural-bamboo axis, which documents native Amazonian *Guadua* species and their assessment for construction (CONTECC 2025 and BJB 2023 records in the bamboo axis): the evidence in this axis qualifies the ecological context from which Amazonian bamboo biomass originates — the interface is stated here as a territorial warning, without creating an authorial section or transferring coefficients.

## 5. Bioeconomy, sociobioeconomy, and value-chain governance

### 5.1. The Brazilian bioeconomy in figures

The [article by Silva, Pereira, and Martins (2018)](silva-pereira-martins-2018-bioeconomia-numeros.md) (*BNDES Setorial*, no. 47, pp. 277–332, Mar. 2018) is an **institutional documentary record**: it estimates sales from economic activities attributable to biomass based on the OECD's global input-output database (2016 version), adapting the approach of Heijman, Piotrowski, and collaborators. The calculated value is **an attribution of sales, not a direct measurement of value added or socioenvironmental impact**. Results: worldwide sales attributable to the Brazilian bioeconomy of US$ 326,1 billion; production carried out in Brazil of US$ 285,9 billion (13,8% of Brazilian GDP in 2016, as an order-of-magnitude comparison that does not transform sales into a direct contribution to GDP) and US$ 40,2 billion in other countries; secondary and tertiary sectors in Brazil with US$ 101,4 billion (2,6% of local production in these sectors); food, beverages, and tobacco with US$ 65,2 billion; in the distribution, agriculture with 53%, food, beverages, and tobacco with 20%, and sectors in other countries with 12%. The methodology **does not disaggregate the Amazon, gender, employment, emissions, or territorial distribution** and does not permit sustainability to be inferred merely from the use of biomass.

### 5.2. Sociobioeconomy and social technology

The [article by Rodrigues et al. (2024)](sociobioeconomia-tecnologia-social-amazonia-2024.md) (*Revista de Administração Contemporânea*, vol. 28, no. 6, e240223) is **theoretical-empirical**: it proposes a framework integrating sociobioeconomy — a counter-hegemonic conception advocated by Amazonian social movements (CNS, COIAB, and CONAQ) — with the social-technology approach, anchored in the concept of a social technological system (Thomas, Dagnino, Juárez). The framework links three dimensions (social, bioterritorial, and economic, with 12 properties) to a cross-cutting technological axis, and was applied to the natural rubber chain in Pará (the Poloprobio case, with three integrated social technologies: Encauchados de Vegetais da Amazônia, Cernambi Virgem Ecológico, and a reapplication methodology).

Case findings: integration of the three social technologies constituted a sociotechnical alliance with multiple actors; handicraft production enabled the socioproductive inclusion of women in the chain; Cernambi Ecológico eliminates the rubber-washing stage (which consumes 20 liters of water per kilogram and generates effluent); and improved pricing for rubber tappers was reported (~R$ 7,41/kg plus a R$ 10/kg bonus for the environmental service of caring for the forest). Limitations recorded in the record: framework validated in a **single case**, qualitative assessment of properties (not all evidenced), and quantitative findings as **interviewee reports, not independent measurements**.

### 5.3. Digital platforms for NTFP chains

The [article by Silva et al. (2025)](plataformas-digitais-bioeconomia-amazonica-2025.md) (*Gestão & Produção*, vol. 32, e14724) is a **systematic literature review** using the protocol of Shaffril et al. (2021): 512 articles identified (Scopus 204, Web of Science 180, SciELO 128), 56 included in the qualitative synthesis (2018–2024, documented numerical flow: 512 → 298 at screening → 164 in full-text review → 56). Findings: blockchain and the Internet of Things improve traceability, authenticity, and sustainability compliance; artificial intelligence supports demand forecasting and market access; platforms such as Origens Brasil connect small producers in conservation areas to global markets, with reported impacts for açaí and Brazil nuts. The proposed framework has four pillars: traceability and authentication; demand forecasting and market management; community capacity-building and empowerment; environmental monitoring and sustainability certification. Declared persistent barriers: limited internet coverage, unstable energy supply, and lack of investment in training — with the risk, noted by the authors, that technological solutions may **reinforce inequalities and exclude more vulnerable communities** without targeted public policies. Central limitation: **framework not empirically tested**, based exclusively on secondary data; the authors recommend pilots and longitudinal studies.

### 5.4. Status of this axis

**Moderate confidence** (reproducible systematic review and institutional documentary record); **theoretical-empirical with a single case** (sociobioeconomy). The records support: (i) the Brazilian bioeconomy is measurable at the macro scale, but attributed sales are not equivalent to GDP or socioenvironmental benefit — and the measurement does not disaggregate the Amazon; (ii) sociobioeconomy can be analytically operationalized through a social technological system; (iii) digital platforms have potential for traceability and market access, conditional on infrastructure and public policies. The axis **does not support**: demonstrated effectiveness of digital platforms (no pilots), or generalization of the sociobioeconomy framework (single case, self-reported indicators).

## 6. Integrated systems and soil quality

The [article by Souza et al. (2024)](souza-et-al-2024-solo-sistemas-integrados.md) (*Forests*, vol. 15, no. 7, art. 1078) is **quantitative field research** in Pindaré-Mirim, Maranhão (eastern Amazon), comparing four land-use systems — perennial *Urochloa brizantha* cv. Marandu pasture (PAST); crop–livestock–forest integration with eucalyptus in rows (ILPF-I); an integrated system with native babassu (*Attalea speciosa*), maize, and *Megathyrsus maximus* (ILPF-II); and maize–grass intercropping without a tree component (MMC) — in a completely randomized design with three replicates, sampling in 2017 and 2018 at depths of 0–0,10 m, 0,10–0,20 m, 0,20–0,30 m, and 0,30–0,50 m, with 95% confidence intervals and principal component analysis.

Results: the ILPF-II system, with babassu, showed **greater organic matter in the surface layer, higher pH, and lower potential acidity** at the evaluated depths; perennial pasture showed higher values of nitrogen, potassium, calcium, sum of bases, and cation-exchange capacity; the multivariate analysis distinguished the systems and associated ILPF-II with organic matter and pH, while pasture was associated with immediate nutrient availability. The authors emphasize that the duration of system adoption influences the soil-fertility response. As the record notes, **the results do not establish the universal superiority of one system**: different indicators favored different systems. Limitations: small number of systems and replicates, local scope, differences in history and establishment time, and a two-year period — causal attributions and generalizations must be made with caution.

**Status of this axis — moderate confidence** (field research, local scope): supports that integrated systems with a native tree component improve indicators of soil chemical quality in an eastern Amazon context; **does not support** the universal superiority of a production arrangement, nor evidence concerning bamboo (the record explicitly notes that the article does not study bamboo).

## 7. Carbon in restoration: the historical methodological reference (2004)

The [dissertation by Martins (2004)](martins-2004-carbono-matas-ciliares.md) (UFSCar, PhD in Science, Ecology and Natural Resources; 161 pages) is the axis's **historical and methodological antecedent**: it estimated carbon sequestration potential through riparian-forest recovery in the São Carlos-SP region (Mogi-Guaçu basin), in the context of the Clean Development Mechanism, using inventory, allometric equations, and the CO2FIX model over a 30-year horizon. Results: an estimated stock of approximately **95 tC/ha** in the compartments considered (above- and belowground; litter, deadwood, and soil carbon are not included in the main estimate); calculated cost of around **US$ 7,20 per tCO₂ equivalent**; restoration of 885 ha of APP estimated at 84.471 tC; small-scale scenario configured with 700 ha, 66.507 tC, and a mean of 2.216 tC/year over 30 years; economic analysis with a price of US$ 5 per emissions-reduction certificate, resulting in **insufficient revenue** relative to the estimated cost under those assumptions.

As the record notes, the results depend on the inventory, equations, included compartments, time horizon, and economic conditions of 2004; CDM costs, rules, and prices **must not be treated as current market guidance**, and the values **cannot be automatically transferred to bamboo or other production systems**. **Status of this axis — low confidence** for current figures (a 2004 snapshot): the document's value is methodological — it makes explicit the difference between biophysical potential and financial viability and establishes the Repository's principle of systemic rigor: the climate value of a resource cannot be separated from the species, territory, measured compartments, management, and economic conditions.

## Convergences in the documentary corpus

- **Recurrent fire degrades the forest and favors bamboo dominance in Acre.** Two independent records — dissertation ([Silva 2017](silva-2017-dinamica-incendios-acre.md)) and article ([Silva et al. 2017](silva-et-al-2017-incendios-dominancia-bambu.md)) — converge through complementary methods (33 years of remote sensing + inventories; 2005/2010 plots): reduction of the tree component, elevated mortality, canopy homogenization, and expansion of *Guadua* (7–9× in the dissertation; 2 → ~15 culms/25 m² in the article; mortality 8% → 50%).
- **Pyrogenic carbon and anthropogenic soils as references for fertility and restoration.** [Maia et al. (2021)](maia-biochar-eucalipto-2021.md) anchors the conceptual origin of biochar in Amazonian Dark Earths; [de Freitas et al. (2026)](dark-earths-crescimento-arvores-2026.md) demonstrates in the field the living microbiological mechanism of these soils — conceptual convergence across two decades and two types of source.
- **Bioeconomy is a contested concept and depends on territorial governance, not only the scale of biomass.** [Silva, Pereira, and Martins (2018)](silva-pereira-martins-2018-bioeconomia-numeros.md) separates economic scale from socioenvironmental performance; [Rodrigues et al. (2024)](sociobioeconomia-tecnologia-social-amazonia-2024.md) proposes community leadership and endogenous bio-inputs; [Silva et al. (2025)](plataformas-digitais-bioeconomia-amazonica-2025.md) makes digital platforms conditional on infrastructure and public policies — three independent records converge on the centrality of governance and value distribution.
- **Territorial evidence before sustainability claims.** [Martins (2004)](martins-2004-carbono-matas-ciliares.md) (nontransferable values), [Bologna and Aquino (2020)](bologna-aquino-2020-desmatamento-populacao.md) (model result, not forecast), [Souza et al. (2024)](souza-et-al-2024-solo-sistemas-integrados.md) (local scope), and the fire records (diagnosis, without automatic transfer) converge on the same methodological principle.
- **Reviews and models state their own limits.** Matarru and Shin (2025) is not a systematic review; Silva et al. (2025) did not test the framework; Bologna and Aquino (2020) does not specify a technological mechanism; Maia et al. (2021) identifies standardization gaps — the collection consistently records the nature and limits of each source.

## 8. Controversies and gaps

### Controversies

1. **Biochar as a climate solution: potential or condition?** [Matarru and Shin (2025)](ficha-biochar-negative-emissions-review.md) presents biochar as a negative-emissions pathway, but makes this potential conditional on LCA boundaries, permanence, coproduct use, and sustainable biomass origin; [Maia et al. (2021)](maia-biochar-eucalipto-2021.md) shows partially positive agronomic results (common bean, seedlings) and no growth effect through 18 months in the Paraná plantation. The corpus **does not support** automatic negative emissions or universal agronomic gains.

2. **Dark earth: nutrients or microbiome?** [de Freitas et al. (2026)](dark-earths-crescimento-arvores-2026.md) demonstrates microbiome restructuring and raises a negative feedback — high nutrient content may reduce the need for nitrogen fixation, challenging the prevailing view that ADE's effect depends equally on nutrients and microbiota; the balance was not quantified (total nitrogen not measured).

3. **Bamboo abundance: resource or sign of degradation?** The fire records ([Silva 2017](silva-2017-dinamica-incendios-acre.md); [Silva et al. 2017](silva-et-al-2017-incendios-dominancia-bambu.md)) show that *Guadua* expansion accompanies tree mortality and canopy homogenization; the forest-type transition hypothesis remains unproven. The interpretation of a "sustainable surplus" for exploitation **finds no documentary support** in the collection.

4. **Is US$ 285,9 billion "the GDP of the Brazilian bioeconomy"?** [Silva, Pereira, and Martins (2018)](silva-pereira-martins-2018-bioeconomia-numeros.md) is clear: these are sales attributed through input-output analysis, an order-of-magnitude comparison with GDP (13,8%), **not** value added, net wealth, or environmental benefit; and the methodology does not disaggregate the Amazon.

5. **Digital platforms: inclusion or exclusion?** [Silva et al. (2025)](plataformas-digitais-bioeconomia-amazonica-2025.md) notes the risk that technological solutions will reinforce inequalities without targeted public policies; the framework was not empirically tested, and the 56 studies include diverse contexts (including China and South Africa), with limited transferability to the Amazon.

6. **Probability of collapse below 10%: forecast or exercise?** [Bologna and Aquino (2020)](bologna-aquino-2020-desmatamento-populacao.md) is modeling with simplifying assumptions (constant rates, aggregate forest, energy as a proxy for technology, abstract technological threshold); the figure is a **model result**, and the article does not demonstrate the effectiveness of any technology.

### Gaps

1. **Long-term field effectiveness of biochar** — the reported trials extend to 18 months ([Maia et al. 2021](maia-biochar-eucalipto-2021.md)); there is no 3–5-year follow-up in Amazonian soils and plantations.
2. **Analytical standardization and regulation of biochar in Brazil** — absence of Brazilian quality criteria, reference materials, and regulation; interlaboratory study with only pH and carbon acceptable ([Maia et al. 2021](maia-biochar-eucalipto-2021.md)).
3. **LCA, permanence, and biochar emissions accounting** — a gap identified in the review by [Matarru and Shin (2025)](ficha-biochar-negative-emissions-review.md); without explicit boundaries there is no basis for a carbon-credit claim.
4. **Dark earth as an inoculant: long term, dose, scale, and site preservation** — 180 days, single dose, one site; management and preservation of archaeological/anthropogenic sites not discussed ([de Freitas et al. 2026](dark-earths-crescimento-arvores-2026.md)).
5. **Updated fire series and continuous monitoring in Acre** — the dissertation covers 1984–2016 ([Silva 2017](silva-2017-dinamica-incendios-acre.md)); there is no record with later data.
6. **Validation of the forest-type transition hypothesis** — transformation into open forest with dominant bamboo is the authors' hypothesis ([Silva et al. 2017](silva-et-al-2017-incendios-dominancia-bambu.md)) and requires confirmation.
7. **Current economic viability of carbon projects in restoration** — the only estimate is from 2004, with outdated CDM assumptions and insufficient revenue ([Martins 2004](martins-2004-carbono-matas-ciliares.md)).
8. **Territorial (Amazon) and social disaggregation of bioeconomy measurement** — the BNDES methodology does not disaggregate the Amazon, gender, employment, or emissions ([Silva, Pereira, and Martins 2018](silva-pereira-martins-2018-bioeconomia-numeros.md)).
9. **Empirical testing of the digital-platform framework** — no pilots in specific communities ([Silva et al. 2025](plataformas-digitais-bioeconomia-amazonica-2025.md)).
10. **Generalization of the sociobioeconomy framework and independent measurement of indicators** — single case; prices and bonuses are reported by interviewees ([Rodrigues et al. 2024](sociobioeconomia-tecnologia-social-amazonia-2024.md)).

## 9. Final synthesis and placement in the Repository

The documentary corpus supports four findings. **First:** recurrent fire is a degradation process that reduces the tree component and favors bamboo dominance in Acre's open forests — convergent empirical evidence between dissertation and article ([Silva 2017](silva-2017-dinamica-incendios-acre.md); [Silva et al. 2017](silva-et-al-2017-incendios-dominancia-bambu.md)), with the territorial warning that bamboo abundance is not sustainable surplus. **Second:** pyrogenic carbon — from Amazonian Dark Earths to biochar — is a common thread in the axis, with conceptual convergence ([Maia et al. 2021](maia-biochar-eucalipto-2021.md)) and empirical demonstration of a microbiological mechanism ([de Freitas et al. 2026](dark-earths-crescimento-arvores-2026.md)), but without long-term evidence, standardization, or emissions accounting. **Third:** the Amazon bioeconomy is conditional on governance, infrastructure, and value distribution — macro measurement without territorial disaggregation ([Silva, Pereira, and Martins 2018](silva-pereira-martins-2018-bioeconomia-numeros.md)), sociobioeconomy as an operationalizable political construction ([Rodrigues et al. 2024](sociobioeconomia-tecnologia-social-amazonia-2024.md)), and digital platforms as an untested promise with exclusion risks ([Silva et al. 2025](plataformas-digitais-bioeconomia-amazonica-2025.md)). **Fourth:** the collection contains no basis for three classes of claim — effectiveness of specific technologies inferred from aggregate modeling ([Bologna and Aquino 2020](bologna-aquino-2020-desmatamento-populacao.md)), current financial viability of carbon projects in restoration ([Martins 2004](martins-2004-carbono-matas-ciliares.md)), and universal superiority of integrated systems ([Souza et al. 2024](souza-et-al-2024-solo-sistemas-integrados.md)).

Within the Repository, the axis remains a collection of **evidence attributed to academic, scientific, and institutional sources** — without a dedicated authorial corpus and without its own authorial-agenda section in this state of the art. The sources are heterogeneous in nature, and the text preserves the hierarchy: empirical field data provide stronger support than reviews, models, and documentary records, and no documentary status is equivalent to experimental validation by the Repository. **Curatorial interpretation:** the collection's natural next steps are (i) to convert this state of the art into an Evidence Map, Gap Map, and Research Agenda; (ii) to prioritize source acquisition — or the conduct of original trials — in the most resource-intensive areas: long-term field effectiveness of biochar, Brazilian analytical standardization, LCA with explicit boundaries, scaling of dark earth as an inoculant, and updating fire series; and (iii) to maintain the territorial warning at the interface with the bamboo axis: Amazonian bamboo biomass comes from forests under recurrent fire pressure, and any production chain requires a local assessment of fire history, integrity, and regeneration.

### 9.1. Implications for public policy

The evidence in the axis points to the following areas, **guided by gaps and without attributing effectiveness to policies that the corpus did not evaluate**:

- **Analytical standardization and regulation of biochar in Brazil** — the absence of national quality criteria, reference materials, and regulation is a central gap (gap 2 in section 8). Policies addressing it could differentiate quality and reduce unwarranted carbon-credit claims ([Maia et al. 2021](maia-biochar-eucalipto-2021.md)); this utility is a conditional recommendation, not demonstrated regulatory effectiveness.
- **LCA and emissions accounting with explicit boundaries** — without clear boundaries, verifiable permanence, sustainable biomass origin, and defined coproduct use, the axis provides no basis for carbon credits (gap 3; [Matarru and Shin 2025](ficha-biochar-negative-emissions-review.md)).
- **Territorial and social disaggregation of bioeconomy measurement** — the macro methodology does not disaggregate the Amazon, gender, employment, or emissions (gap 8; [Silva, Pereira, and Martins 2018](silva-pereira-martins-2018-bioeconomia-numeros.md)); investment decisions require a territorial reading and cannot treat attributed sales as GDP or socioenvironmental benefit.
- **Targeted digital-inclusion policies** — digital platforms may reinforce inequalities without infrastructure and targeted public policies; the framework remains untested ([Silva et al. 2025](plataformas-digitais-bioeconomia-amazonica-2025.md)). Sociobioeconomy can be analytically operationalized through social technology, but still lacks independent measurement and comparison across territories (gap 10; [Rodrigues et al. 2024](sociobioeconomia-tecnologia-social-amazonia-2024.md)).
- **Value-chain governance and territoriality** — infrastructure, value distribution, territorial rights, and ecological assessment condition any chain. The warning about post-fire bamboo precludes treating “biomass surplus” as available without a local evaluation of fire history, integrity, and regeneration.

**Curatorial note:** these are gap-driven implications and conditional recommendations, not a ready-made policy, certification, universal guidance, or evidence of public-policy effectiveness.

### 9.2. Material, social, and environmental sustainability

- **Environmental dimension — strong but heterogeneous coverage:** the axis addresses pyrogenic carbon, biochar quality, LCA boundaries, soil indicators, fires, and forest integrity. Even so, **potential is not demonstrated performance**: negative emissions depend on LCA boundaries, permanence, coproducts, and biomass origin ([Matarru and Shin 2025](ficha-biochar-negative-emissions-review.md)).
- **Material dimension — partial coverage:** feedstock, pyrolysis temperature, porosity, and O/C and H/Corg ratios condition biochar quality; these indicators do not authorize transferring performance across feedstocks, reactors, soils, or applications without specific characterization.
- **Social dimension — moderate coverage:** sociobioeconomy, platforms, and NTFP chains are treated as a political and operationalizable construction, but the evidence base includes a single case and price or bonus indicators reported by interviewees, without independent measurement (gap 10).
- **No automatic sustainability:** no record demonstrates that biochar, bamboo, or integrated systems are automatically sustainable. Renewable origin does not guarantee negative emissions, social benefit, absence of contaminants, or universal superiority ([Souza et al. 2024](souza-et-al-2024-solo-sistemas-integrados.md); [Silva et al. 2017](silva-et-al-2017-incendios-dominancia-bambu.md)).
- **Variables still required:** process energy and fuel, transport, emissions, contaminants and heavy metals, water, waste, effect durability, carbon permanence, coproduct use, and end of life must be defined by system and functional unit.

**Curatorial limit:** concluding that “Amazonian biochar or bamboo automatically offsets carbon” would extrapolate beyond the corpus; the claim would require LCA with explicit boundaries, verified permanence, and long-term field series.

### 9.3. Transfer limits and separation among evidence, hypothesis, and agenda

The evidence refers to localized studies, reviews, and models, not universal laws:

- **Heterogeneous nature of the sources:** empirical field data provide stronger support than reviews, models, and documentary records; the weight of each source type is stated in the executive summary and thematic sections.
- **Specific territories:** fires and bamboo dominance refer to Acre; dark earth, to one Amazonian site; integrated systems, to Pindaré-Mirim/MA; deforestation modeling is global and parametric. Territorial transferability is limited (gap 6).
- **A model or estimate is not equivalent to an implemented policy:** [Bologna and Aquino (2020)](bologna-aquino-2020-desmatamento-populacao.md) presents a model result, not a demonstration of technological effectiveness; [Martins (2004)](martins-2004-carbono-matas-ciliares.md) is an estimate based on 2004 assumptions, not automatically transferable to current markets.
- **Materials and components:** no conclusion about biochar, bamboo, or integrated systems automatically transfers to another territory, species, soil, scale, reactor, or product without local validation.

| Layer | What the axis allows us to say | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Evidence** | Findings attributed to full sources and their respective methods and scopes | recurrent fire favors *Guadua* dominance in Acre; ADE increased seedling growth at the dose and site studied; biochar quality depends on feedstock and pyrolysis; US$ 285,9 billion represents attributed sales, not GDP |
| **Hypothesis** | Plausible relationships not yet demonstrated at the necessary scope | negative emissions from biochar; forest transition with dominant bamboo; generalizable sociobioeconomy; digital platforms promoting inclusion |
| **Agenda** | Necessary investigations, pilots, and data | biochar standardization and regulation; explicit LCA; territorial measurement; scaling dark earth as an inoculant; updated fire series; social and environmental monitoring of platforms and chains |

### 9.4. Documentary references, rights, and cross-axis connections

- **Distinct documentary natures:** articles, dissertations, narrative or systematic reviews, models, and institutional records are not equivalent evidence. Frameworks and policies cited by the records are documentary context, not proof of implementation or effectiveness.
- **Rights review:** this state-of-the-art review does not redistribute protected full sources. The links point to the Repository's own analyses; rights to the works remain with their holders. Any expanded use of a standard, proprietary methodology, or institutional document requires checking the version, validity, scope, official endpoint, and legitimate access conditions.
- **Connections already documented:** the interface with structural bamboo is a territorial warning supported by the records on fires and *Guadua* dominance; it is not equivalent to validating an Amazonian bamboo production chain.
- **Relationship with the authorial corpus:** [Notebook 6 — Biomass biochar and cascading use of bamboo](../visao-do-autor/caderno-06-biochar-pirolise-uso-cascata.md) reorganizes, as an authorial hypothesis and agenda, themes also examined in this state-of-the-art review — pyrolysis, biochar quality, carbon permanence, LCA, co-products, and cascading use. The relationship is one of context and authorial derivation: the Notebook is not included in this review's evidence count, does not replace the source records, and does not demonstrate net carbon removal, agronomic safety, or the performance of a specific system.
- **Candidate connections:** land reform/agroforestry, social housing, vernacular architecture, and FPIC are future avenues for context or agenda. They will be classified in the general cross-reference map without automatic equivalence and without attributing content to the axis that it does not contain.
